## I Conception of the Anti-Terrorism Act of 2020

On May 23, 2017, Marawi City was attacked by armed men.[^1] This resulted in armed confrontation between government forces and pro-ISIS militants. A day later, then-President Duterte put the entire region under martial law.[^2] When the Mindanao-wide martial law was approaching its expiration date, the Congress introduced House Bill (HB) No. 6875 or the proposed Anti-Terrorism Act of 2020. On June 1, 2020, then-President Duterte wrote a letter to House Speaker Alan Peter Cayetano saying HB No. 6875 must be immediately passed "to address the urgent need to strengthen the law on anti-terrorism" and "effectively contain the menace of terrorist acts."[^3] This allowed the House to pass the bill on 2nd reading as well as 3rd and final reading on the same day.[^4]

## II Birth of the Anti-Terrorism Act of 2020

On July 3, 2020, then-President Duterte signed this controversial bill into law.[^5] The law will take effect after publication pending the issuance of implementing rules and regulations.

## III About the Anti-Terrorism Act of 2020

Republic Act No. 11479, or the Anti-Terrorism Act of 2020 (ATA), repealed the Human Security Act of 2007. The ATA aims (1) to protect life, liberty, and property from terrorism; (2) to condemn terrorism as inimical and dangerous to national security of the country and welfare of the people; and (3) to make any form of terrorism a crime against Filipino people, against humanity and against the law of Nations.

Terrorism can be committed by any person, here in the Philippines or abroad, regardless of the stage of execution if that person commits the following acts:

(1) cause death or serious bodily injury to any person, or endangers a person’s life[^6]; (2) cause extensive damage or destruction to a government or public facility, public place, or private property[^7]; (3) cause extensive interference with, damage, or destruction to critical infrastructure[^8]; (4) Develops, manufactures, possesses, acquires, transports, supplies or uses weapons, explosives or of biological, nuclear, radiological, or chemical weapons[^9]; (5) Release of dangerous substances, or causing fire, floods, or explosions[^10]. The purpose of the act should be to intimidate the public, create an atmosphere or spread a message of fear, provoke or to influence by intimidation the government or any international organization, or seriously destabilize or destroy the fundamental, political, economic, or social structures of the country, or create a public emergency or undermine public safety.[^11] ATA qualifies that advocacy, protest, dissent, stoppage of work, industrial or mass action, creative, artistic, and cultural expression, or other similar exercises of civil and political rights are acts that are not considered as terrorism.[^12]

The ATA also penalizes threat to commit terrorism[^13], planning, training, preparing and facilitating the commission of terrorism[^14], conspiracy to commit terrorism[^15], proposal to commit terrorism[^16], inciting to commit terrorism[^17], recruitment to and membership in a terrorist group, and providing material support to terrorists[^18].

Moreover, ATA allows longer period and extended coverage of surveillance[^19], designation and freezing of assets of designated terrorist individual, groups of persons, organizations, or associations[^20], restriction on the right to travel[^21], and deletion of award of damages to persons acquitted of charges of terrorism[^22].

Finally, the heart and soul of the Anti-Terrorism Act (ATA) is found in its Section 29, which authorizes the Anti-Terrorism Council (ATC) to order an arrest without judicial warrant, and to extend detention without judicial charge beyond the reglementary 3-day period under the Revised Penal Code (RPC). According to Senator Panfilo Lacson, the principal author of the ATA, the rationale behind this provision is that law enforcement agents need at least 14 days to develop a case and to file a strong case for violation of ATA to strengthen the case. Defense Secretary Delfin Lorenzana echoed this. He said that longer detention period is necessary so that the authorities can hold the him if he is really a terrorist. This will also disable him from committing a terror act.

## IV Petition

The 37 petitions were filed by framers of the Constitution, law experts, and human rights advocates, as well as individuals who claim to have been victims of authorities’ terrorist-tagging.[^23]

The major issues raised are as follows:

1. Is the definition of terrorism under the Anti-Terrorism Act of 2020 ambiguous?

2. Was the Anti-Terrorism Council given too much power?

3. Is the warrantless arrest and detention without charges of suspected terrorists for up to 24 days contrary to the Constitution, the Revised Penal Code, the Rules of Court, and international obligations against arbitrary detention?

4. Did the sections on surveillance violate the people’s rights to due process and to privacy of communication and correspondence, rights to freedom of speech, expression, and religion, right against unreasonable searches and seizures, and the accused’s right to presumption of innocence?

## V Decision of the Supreme Court

In December 2021, the Philippine Supreme Court has mostly upheld the heavily contested anti-terror law, including the 24-day detention power.[^24] In justifying its ruling, the Supreme Court accepted the interpretation of the Solicitor General. The Solicitor General in his comment stated that Section 29 does not authorize the ATC to order the arrest of any person. The Solicitor General interpreted Section 29 as referring to warrantless arrests under Rule 113 of the Rules of Court. Simply put, Section 29 is just a reiteration of Rule 113. The Supreme Court declared only two parts unconstitutional, part of the so-called killer caveat under Section 4(e) and the second mode of designation under Section 25.[^25] Section 4 (e) deals with excluding mass actions and similar exercise of civil and political rights from the definition of terrorism while Section 25 is about requests by foreign agencies or bodies to designate persons as terrorists and terrorist organizations.[^26]

The Supreme Court voted 12-3 to declare unconstitutional the qualifier portion of Section 4 that states “which are not intended to cause death or serious physical harm to a person, to endanger a person's life, or to create a serious risk to public safety".[^27] This qualifier to the proviso in Section 4 "is declared as unconstitutional for being overbroad and violative of freedom of expression".[^28]

By a vote of 9-6, a portion of Section 25, paragraph 2 was also declared unconstitutional. This provision allows "request for designations by other jurisdictions or supranational jurisdictions may be adopted by the ATC (Anti-Terrorism Council) after determination that the proposed designee meets the criteria for designation of UNSCR No. 1373".[^29]

On April 26, 2022, the high tribunal denied “with finality” the appeals to reverse its decision on petitions challenging the anti-terror law because of “lack of substantial issues and arguments raised by the petitioners”.[^30]

## V After Effects

On March 2, 2022, 26 groups filed a joint motion for reconsideration. The petitioners cited the recent killing of Lumad school volunteer teacher Chad Booc and four others in New Bataan, Davao de Oro. Booc has long been subjected to red-tagging by government officials before his death. Also, the motion noted that red-tagging has in many instances preceded the extrajudicial killings of activists and human rights defenders, with Booc, also a petitioner against ATA, being one of the victims paraded as ‘terrorists’ by the military.[^31]

On June 25, 2022, ATC Resolution No. 31 designated Luis Jalandoni as a terrorist, along with 10 other persons with alleged links with the Communist Party of the Philippines - New Peoples’ Army (CPP-NPA), Abu Sayyaf Group (ASG), and Daulah Islamiyah (DI). ATC Resolution No. 31 was signed on May 25, 2022.[^32]

On June 22, 2022, Outgoing National Security Adviser Hermogenes Esperon Jr. managed to get the National Telecommunications Commission (NTC) to block access to 28 websites.[^33] This was made possible despite not citing evidence. In particular, his letter of request to the NTC failed to cite even the specific articles of the ATA that he claims these websites violated. A month later, opposition party-lists filed a bill to repeal the Anti-Terrorism Act. In filing the bill, Rep. Raoul Manuel (Kabataan party-list), Rep. France Castro (ACT Teachers party-list) and Rep. Arlene Brosas (Gabriela Women’s Party) said "it is high time for Congress to do its part to ensure that this bill will not have the capability to harm any other person in the country."[^34] However, this ship may not likely sail considering that the House of Representatives has historically been dominated by members aligned with or supportive of the sitting president.

## VI Conclusion

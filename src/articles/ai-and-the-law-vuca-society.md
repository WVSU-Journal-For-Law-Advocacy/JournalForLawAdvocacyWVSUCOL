I served as a volunteer arbitrator in the latest edition of the Willem C. Vis East Moot Competition in the first week of April 2025, prior to the original competition in Vienna, Austria. Suppose there was one notable feature of both sister competitions. In that case, it’s the fact that Jus Mundi supports the moot by providing arbitrators, coaches, and mooters free access to the legal research version of Jus Mundi.

Jus Mundi is a legal technology company that provides a smart research engine for international law and arbitration. It is a lesser-known competitor to LexisNexis and Westlaw. But for the arbitration community, it is much more of a byword than its next competitor, Wolters Kluwer. Its headquarters are located in both the posh cities of Paris and New York.

Jus Mundi, together with its competitors, aims to provide a comprehensive legal database containing a vast collection of legal treaties on International Arbitration, Public International Law, treaties, arbitral awards of institutions and ad hoc arbitrations, judgments, procedural orders, and even pleadings. To up the ante, Jus Mundi has partnered with the International Chamber of Commerce to provide its publications and publicized awards. Partnerships were also forged with the International Bar Association, the International Center for Dispute Resolution, among others, to provide users with unprecedented access to arbitration awards, case documents, and institutional practice notes. It has rolled out Jus Connect, a professional networking platform for all arbitration practitioners to connect and conduct due diligence among its members. The professional networking platform is nearly akin to a social network site like Facebook or LinkedIn, only that its members are vetted or pre-screened arbitration practitioners like counsels, arbitrators and tribunal secretaries.

And with the foray of Generative Artificial Intelligence, which we call Gen-AI for short, Jus Mundi now introduces “Jus AI”. The rise of globalization, geopolitical tensions, and intricate supply chains has created interconnected economies in which one part of the world sneezes, and the other part sneezes in response. Disruptions or revolutions can cascade rapidly across sectors of this planet. Its effects cascade as quickly as legacies vanish at the bat of an eyelash.

Hence, the term VUCA was born. VUCA stands for Volatility, Uncertainty, Complexity, and Ambiguity, representing the increasingly dynamic and unpredictable environment in which individuals and organizations operate. VUCA has routinely posed significant challenges to leaders who navigate uncertainties, especially when the organization involved is perceived to be a top-tier institution in their field.

VUCA has the potential to erode that competitive advantage for those institutions that are not vigilant. And the legal profession, together with its institutions, is not exempt. AI, when read together with VUCA, transforms our field’s operational processes. AI automates at such speed that it reduces the need for manpower at unprecedented rates.

The consensus among thought leaders is that lawyers who strategically leverage AI will do better in the long run than those legal professionals who vehemently resist this development. We have seen this in history: what happens to companies that refuse innovations. Generative AI does not only answer inquiries similar to those of anyone who googles research ideas to sound like an expert. AI automates several mundane tasks that would ordinarily be painstaking and time-consuming. Generative AI tools, when maximized to their capacity, can streamline processes leading to enhanced efficiency, accuracy, and strategic decision-making spanning a significant number of legal tasks.

Among the key features that lead to enhanced efficiency are the following:

● Time-Savings on Repetitive Tasks: This is particularly evident in tasks like document review, where AI can process and analyze vast quantities of legal documents far more quickly than human counterparts, identifying relevant information and patterns (Wang, 2025).

● Trimming Errors: By automating data extraction and analysis, AI significantly reduces the potential for human error in tasks such as due diligence and contract review, ensuring greater accuracy in legal proceedings (Bauer et al., 2025).

● Improved Legal Research: AI algorithms can meticulously review documents for inconsistencies or omissions, significantly reducing human error in contracts and legal briefs (“Augmenting Legal Expertise: The Role of AI in Enhancing Human Capabilities in Legal Services,” 2025).

● Better Automated Workflows: AI-powered platforms can automate the routing of documents, scheduling of appointments, and management of deadlines, streamlining administrative burdens for legal professionals (Jaiswal, 2024).

● Predictive Analytics: AI can integrate with existing legal tech to create seamless, automated sequences for tasks such as drafting standard legal documents, managing case files, and scheduling, thereby freeing up legal professionals for more complex analytical work.

The key features translate to better value delivery and a competitive advantage within the industry. Last 1 April 2025, this author attended a Jus Mundi, a legal technology company, event held at the Hong Kong International Arbitration Centre (“HKIAC”). Jus Mundi leads a discussion on how artificial intelligence is not merely a tool but a transformative force capable of reshaping legal practice by automating routine tasks and improving overall productivity.

AI-powered solutions can streamline processes such as document review, legal research, and contract analysis, thereby enabling legal professionals to allocate their efforts to more complex, high-value intellectual endeavors (Weichbroth, 2025). This augmentation of human capabilities, rather than outright replacement, underscores a paradigm shift in which AI serves as a powerful assistant, enhancing the precision and breadth of legal analysis while freeing up practitioners for tasks requiring creative and social intelligence (Artiaga and Jomilla, 2025; “Augmenting Legal Expertise: The Role of AI in Enhancing Human Capabilities in Legal Services,” 2025).

GenAI is indeed transformative, enhancing efficiency, accuracy, and strategic decision-making in legal tasks that were unimaginable when this author began her legal career. For example, the application of AI in document analysis and review significantly reduces the time and error-proneness associated with traditional manual methods, allowing swift scanning and analysis of extensive legal documents, contracts, and agreements to identify pertinent information, potential risks, and compliance issues (Jaiswal, 2024; Madaoui, 2024).

AI is reshaping the law on all fronts, from education to the courtroom. New teaching methodologies, pedagogy and curriculum adjustments are indeed needed to prepare students for an AI-integrated legal environment. This includes fostering critical thinking skills necessary to evaluate AI outputs, manage biases, and address ethical considerations such as "hallucinations" in AI-generated content (G and Nair, 2024).

Furthermore, integrating AI tools into legal education curricula enables future lawyers to develop technical literacy and critical thinking skills essential for navigating the data-driven legal marketplace (“The Evolving Role of Artificial Intelligence in Legal Education and Research,” 2025).

Shirley Tung and the rest of the panelists had underscored that AI has the potential to accelerate learning and career advancement for students and early-career legal practitioners. In the middle of the discussion, the panelists explained in detail that instead of doing the rigorous and time-consuming activity of documentation and compiling legal research, junior associates can perform the tasks the equivalent of a junior partner. This transformation is particularly evident in law case analysis, where AI systems can create immersive, interactive learning environments, guiding students through interdisciplinary and cross-cultural perspectives (Wang, Xu and Xu, 2024).

That said, the discussion highlighted that the future legal landscape will require lawyers proficient in both the law and adept at collaborating with intelligent machines. Certain positions, like paralegals, were said to disappear in the near future, as this can be performed by bots. New positions will emerge, however. These positions are related to technology, such as prompt engineers or specialists, and their emergence will be seen in the bigger law firms. These emerging roles underscore a critical shift in legal practice, demanding a workforce capable of leveraging AI for sophisticated legal analysis and strategic problem-solving rather than rote tasks (Tung et al., 2025).

This evolution necessitates a re-evaluation of current legal education paradigms, pushing for curricula that not only instill traditional legal principles but also cultivate technological fluency and ethical reasoning concerning AI applications (G and Nair, 2024; “The Evolving Role of Artificial Intelligence in Legal Education and Research,” 2025).

But this is not to say that GenAI is foolproof. It does have its fair share of challenges and limitations that educators and practitioners must address critically (Yu, 2023). There are ethical considerations and practical difficulties that come with integrating AI into arbitration and legal education. There were questions about how much arbitrators can delegate to AI tools within their mandate to decide a case. The principle of party autonomy allows parties to choose an arbitrator because of his qualifications. The parties' power to select an arbitrator is based on consent. Consent is the bedrock of arbitration. When an arbitrator delegates the power vested in him by virtue of the parties’ agreement, questions arise about the enforceability of the final arbitral award it issues. This ethical dilemma is further complicated by concerns regarding data privacy, algorithmic bias, and the potential for AI systems to "hallucinate" or generate inaccurate legal information, which could undermine the integrity of arbitral proceedings (Wilinski and Durbas, 2023; Khurramov et al., 2025).

The inherent "black box" nature of many AI tools further exacerbates these concerns, making it challenging to scrutinize the precise mechanisms by which AI arrives at its conclusions and hindering the provision of transparent justifications for decisions (Bauer et al., 2025). Algorithmic bias in Large Language Models (LLMs) poses a significant challenge, as these models may perpetuate and amplify biases embedded in their training data, potentially leading to unfair or discriminatory outcomes in legal analysis and decision-making (Bauer et al., 2025). This results in the generation of plausible but inaccurate information. The challenge with LLMs is the limited availability of accurate training data for specialized arbitration AI tools due to the confidentiality of proceedings.

There is no turning back from AI. The rise of legal tech companies such as Jus Mundi, Kluwer Arbitration, and other platforms offering sophisticated AI-powered arbitration tools signals a paradigm shift in the legal industry, necessitating continuous adaptation and innovation from legal professionals (Tung et al., 2025).

The integration of AI, especially in international arbitration, introduces novel ethical dilemmas concerning transparency, data security, and the potential for AI-generated content to influence critical legal decisions (Quiroga, 2023; Gómez, 2024). This paradigm shift necessitates a rigorous examination of how AI tools are vetted and whether they possess sufficient privacy and security standards to protect sensitive arbitral data (Bauer et al., 2025).

Furthermore, the "black box" nature of advanced AI models presents a significant hurdle for ensuring due process and the ability to challenge AI-driven outcomes, as the reasoning behind their conclusions often remains opaque to human review (Vy, 2023).

A valuable resource in this regard is the *Guidelines on the Use of AI in Arbitration* promulgated by the Chartered Institute of Arbitrators (Bauer et al., 2025). This document frames artificial intelligence as a pivotal instrument that augments efficiency, precision, and strategic discernment in legal practice. Amid the Volatile, Uncertain, Complex, and Ambiguous milieu, legal professionals must remain attuned to this emergent technology, exercising vigilance to avert the erosion of competitive advantages and cultivating acumen to address novel challenges. Accordingly, CIArb has formulated a comprehensive guideline to navigate these exigencies within dispute resolution contexts (Bauer et al., 2025).

The VUCA world, characterized by Volatility, Uncertainty, Complexity, and Ambiguity, poses significant challenges across industries, and the legal profession is no exception. This environment demands vigilance against the erosion of competitive advantage and the ability to navigate novel challenges proactively (Kruger and Barkhuizen, 2024). The rapid emergence and integration of AI tools, while offering unprecedented efficiencies, exacerbate the problems brought about by VUCA, creating a “pacing problem” where technological advancements outpace the corresponding development of legal and ethical frameworks.

CIArb astutely recognizes the pressing imperative for clear guidance and delivers a pioneering response to AI's profound ethical and practical challenges—including algorithmic bias, transparency deficits, data security risks, and the risk of generating inaccurate outputs. However, the guidelines themselves acknowledge ongoing hurdles like the "black box" nature of AI tools, which impedes human oversight, scrutiny of decision mechanisms, and the provision of transparent justifications, potentially risking due process and award enforceability (Bauer et al., 2025).

As such, the Chartered Institute of Arbitrators' Guideline on the Use of AI in Arbitration provides essential direction for dispute resolvers, parties, and their representatives, enabling them to harness AI's benefits while mitigating risks to procedural integrity and the enforceability of awards (Bauer et al., 2025)..

The CIArb AI Guideline aims to provide direction for all participants in arbitration – including dispute resolvers, parties, and their representatives – to harness AI's benefits while safeguarding the integrity of the process, procedural rights, and the enforceability of awards (Bauer et al., 2025). This guideline is not a manual for specific AI tools but rather a set of principles that acknowledge the rapid evolution of technology and the ongoing demand for increased professional efficiency (Bauer et al., 2025).

Here are the following key principles outlined in the Chartered Institute of Arbitrators' Guideline on the Use of AI in Arbitration (Bauer et al., 2025):

## 1. Maintaining Human Responsibility and Independent Judgment

While Generative AI automates tasks and improves research, the CIArb Guideline strongly emphasizes that participants "shall not be freed of its personal obligation to independently analyse the facts, the applicable law, and the evidence" (Bauer et al., 2025). This directly reinforces your point about avoiding reliance on AI to "sound like an expert" without true understanding. For arbitrators, specifically, the guideline states they "should not relinquish their decision-making powers to AI but may use AI to support more accurate and efficient processing of submitted information, always ensuring independent judgment" (Bauer et al., 2025). This means AI should augment, not replace, human intellect and responsibility.

## 2. Verification of AI-Generated Output

A critical aspect of using AI responsibly, especially Generative AI, is the need for rigorous verification. The CIArb Guideline mandates that participants "shall not rely on AI-generated output without having independently verified the relevant sources and accuracy of the obtained outputs" (Bauer et al., 2025). This guideline directly addresses the "Trimming Errors" feature you mentioned, underscoring that while AI can reduce errors, the ultimate responsibility for accuracy rests with the legal professional. Arbitrators are also advised to "independently verify the accuracy and correctness of information obtained through AI" (Bauer et al., 2025).

## 3. No Delegation of Decision-Making to AI

The discussion in your article about AI transforming roles and requiring prompt engineering must be balanced with the understanding that AI is a tool, not a decision-maker. The CIArb Guideline explicitly states that participants "shall not delegate any decision-making responsibilities to the AI Tool and assume paternity and responsibility for the relevant output" (Bauer et al., 2025). Similarly, arbitrators must "assume responsibility for all aspects of an award, regardless of any use of AI to assist with the decision-making process" (Bauer et al., 2025). This principle is vital for maintaining accountability and the ethical framework of legal practice.

## 4. Preserving the Integrity of the Arbitration Process

The overarching concern is that AI tools should not compromise the fundamental principles of justice. The CIArb Guideline warns that participants "shall not use AI Tools in a way that may affect the integrity of the arbitration and/or the validity and enforcement of the resulting award" (Bauer et al., 2025). This aligns with your article's concern about maintaining competitive advantage and avoiding the erosion of institutional standing in a VUCA world.

## 5. Transparency in AI Use

While the CIArb Guideline outlines various aspects, transparency over the use of AI by arbitrators is explicitly mentioned (Bauer et al., 2025). Although not elaborated upon in the provided excerpts, the inclusion of "Transparency over use of AI by arbitrators" as a distinct section within the guideline suggests that disclosure and openness about AI integration are crucial for maintaining trust and fairness in arbitral proceedings.

**The guideline also emphasizes the importance of understanding the specific limitations and potential biases inherent in different AI models to ensure their appropriate and ethical application in complex legal contexts.**

More than anything, there is no question among the panel that this transformation will render traditional legal roles as obsolete, and new ones will foray into the scene. A concept that is now reiterated, that everyone must have in order to fully leverage Generative AI, is prompt engineering. As artificial intelligence relies on algorithms, or a detailed step-by-step instruction for the purposes of fulfilling a specific task, it is essential that the user provide specific, simple language.

The legal profession stands at an undeniable crossroads, where the forces of a Volatile, Uncertain, Complex, and Ambiguous world converge with the transformative power of Artificial Intelligence. As this article has demonstrated, AI—particularly Generative AI—is not merely an incremental technological advancement; rather, it represents a paradigm shift that redefines operational processes, promises unparalleled efficiencies, and demands a fundamental rethinking of legal education and practice. By automating mundane tasks, significantly reducing errors, improving legal research, and enabling predictive analytics, AI tools are poised to enhance value delivery and confer a significant competitive advantage on those who embrace them strategically.
